Signs of low vitamin B12 in vegetarians

Written and reviewed by Shubham Pasalkar, FounderUpdated 29 Oct 2026

Common signs of low B12 are tiredness, pale skin, a sore or swollen tongue, palpitations, and numbness or tingling in the hands and feet. Nerve symptoms can appear before anaemia does. Vegetarians are at higher risk because B12 occurs naturally only in animal foods, so milk, curd, paneer, eggs, fortified foods or a supplement matter.

Adult need (ICMR-NIN)2.2 mcg/day
Adult RDA (NIH)2.4 mcg/day
Upper limitNone set
Key testsB12, MMA, homocysteine

Vitamin B12 helps make red blood cells, DNA and the myelin that protects nerves. Your body cannot make it, and in nature it comes only from animal foods. That is why low B12 is especially common among vegetarians in India. This guide covers the signs, the tests and the realistic sources, using the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements (ODS), ICMR-NIN and Indian studies.

Signs and symptoms of low B12

TypeSigns listed by the NIH
BloodMegaloblastic anaemia (large, abnormal red blood cells), low red or white cells or platelets, pale skin, palpitations
GeneralFatigue, weight loss
MouthGlossitis: a sore, smooth or swollen tongue
NervesNumbness and tingling in the hands and feet
BrainMemory problems and, in severe or long-standing cases, dementia

Two points matter for vegetarians. First, the NIH notes that nerve symptoms can occur without anaemia, so a normal haemoglobin does not rule out low B12. Second, early deficiency is often silent. In a Pune study of 119 young, healthy vegetarian graduates, about half met the definition of B12 deficiency, yet none had clinical signs.

What it looks like in Indian patients

A study of 220 patients with B12 deficiency at hospitals in Varanasi and Lucknow (2015–2020) gives a clear picture:

  • 86% were strict vegetarians.
  • Tingling or pins and needles (paraesthesia) was reported by 98%.
  • Head heaviness or headache affected 95%.
  • 88% had anaemia, and 38% had skin changes.

The authors urge doctors to think of B12 when symptoms are vague, especially in pure vegetarians, because most of the damage is reversible if treated early. Older adults are another risk group: at AIIMS Raebareli, 17% of older patients tested had B12 below 200 pg/mL and another 17% were borderline.

Who is most at risk

  • Lifelong or strict vegetarians and vegans, especially with low milk intake.
  • Pregnant and breastfeeding women. In rural Vellore, 55% of pregnant women had B12 below 200 pg/mL. The NIH notes that exclusively breastfed babies of vegan mothers can also become deficient.
  • Older adults, as the AIIMS Raebareli figures above show.
  • People taking metformin, or acid-reducing medicines (proton pump inhibitors such as omeprazole, and H2 blockers), which can reduce absorption.

Which tests to ask for

A serum B12 test is the usual start, but it is not perfect. The NIH gives these markers:

TestSuggests deficiency
Serum vitamin B12Below 200–250 pg/mL (148–185 pmol/L) in most labs
Methylmalonic acid (MMA)Above 0.271 micromol/L; the most sensitive marker
HomocysteineAbove 15 micromol/L, though other causes exist

If your B12 is in the grey zone (about 150–399 pg/mL), experts suggest checking MMA. The Pune study found that combining B12, holotranscobalamin and homocysteine identified deficiency in Indian vegetarians more accurately than B12 alone.

How much B12 do you need, and from where?

ICMR-NIN sets the adult requirement at 2.2 mcg a day; the NIH RDA is 2.4 mcg (2.6 mcg in pregnancy, 2.8 mcg when breastfeeding). Vegetarian food sources from the NIH table:

FoodB12 per serving
Milk, 1 cup1.3 mcg
Plain yoghurt, 6 oz (about 170 g)1.0 mcg
Fortified breakfast cereal, 1 serving0.6 mcg (varies by brand)
Cheddar cheese, 1.5 oz (about 42 g)0.5 mcg
Egg, 1 large0.5 mcg

Two cups of milk a day get close to the requirement, but many people drink less. Plant foods are not reliable sources. The NIH says fortified foods and supplements substantially reduce the risk for vegetarians. B12 has no upper limit because of its low toxicity. Absorption is efficient at small doses (about 50% at 1–2 mcg) and falls at high doses (about 1.3% at 1,000 mcg), which is why treatment doses are much larger than daily maintenance doses.

If a test shows deficiency, your doctor may prescribe high-dose tablets or injections; follow that plan. A daily supplement is for maintenance, not for correcting a deficiency.

FAQ

What are the first signs of low B12?

Tiredness, pale skin, a sore tongue and tingling in the hands or feet are common early signs. Many people, especially young vegetarians, have low B12 on a blood test with no symptoms at all.

Can vegetarians get enough B12 without supplements?

It is possible with enough milk, curd, cheese or eggs, or with fortified foods: a cup of milk gives about 1.3 mcg of the 2.2–2.4 mcg daily need. Many vegetarians fall short, which is why the NIH says fortified foods and supplements reduce risk.

Which test is best for B12 deficiency?

Serum B12 is the usual first test. If it is borderline, methylmalonic acid (MMA) is the most sensitive marker, and homocysteine can help.

Does metformin cause low B12?

The NIH says metformin might reduce vitamin B12 absorption, as can acid-reducing medicines such as proton pump inhibitors and H2 blockers. If you take them long term, ask your doctor whether to check your B12.
