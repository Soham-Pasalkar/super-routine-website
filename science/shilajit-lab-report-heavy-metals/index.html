How to read a shilajit lab report for heavy metals

Written and reviewed by Shubham Pasalkar, FounderUpdated 27 Oct 2026

A real shilajit lab report (certificate of analysis) names an accredited laboratory, matches your batch number, and gives a measured number in ppm (mg/kg) for lead, arsenic, mercury and cadmium. Limits often cited for herbal products are 10 ppm for lead and arsenic, 1 ppm for mercury and about 0.2–0.3 ppm for cadmium. Lower is better.

Lead, arsenic (herbal limit)10 ppm
Mercury1 ppm
Cadmium≈ 0.2–0.3 ppm
1 ppm equals1 mg per kg

Shilajit is a mineral-rich resin from mountain rock, so it naturally carries whatever metals are in that rock. Many brands now say "lab tested", but a phrase on a label is not a result. This guide shows what a genuine heavy-metal report contains, how to read the numbers, and the red flags that should make you put the jar down.

Why shilajit needs heavy-metal testing

  • A 2024 review in Biological Trace Element Research found around 65 elements reported in shilajit, including toxic metals such as lead, arsenic, cadmium and mercury. Most studies found levels below the permissible limits, but some found them exceeded, and the authors conclude that taking shilajit without knowing its metal levels is not safe.
  • A 2021 analysis of Indian and Pakistani shilajit found lead, arsenic and mercury above permissible limits in the samples tested.
  • The US Department of Defense's Operation Supplement Safety notes that raw shilajit can contain lead, cadmium, arsenic and mercury, and that "without laboratory testing, there is no way to know the actual ingredients."
  • This is not only a shilajit issue. In a 2008 JAMA study, 20.7% of Ayurvedic medicines bought online contained detectable lead, mercury or arsenic, and 75% of those products claimed Good Manufacturing Practices.

The parts of a shilajit lab report

FieldWhat to look for
Laboratory nameAn independent lab, not the brand itself. In India, look for NABL accreditation to ISO/IEC 17025 for the test in question.
Report number and dateRecent, and from before the batch was sold
Sample or batch numberMust match the batch number printed on your pack
MethodAn instrumental method for trace metals such as ICP-MS or ICP-OES
ResultAn actual number for each metal, with units
Limit of quantification (LOQ)The smallest amount the lab can measure. "ND" or "BLQ" means not detected or below that level.
SpecificationThe limit the result is judged against, ideally shown next to it

Reading the numbers

Results are usually in ppm (parts per million, the same as mg/kg) or ppb (parts per billion, µg/kg). 1 ppm = 1,000 ppb. A lead result of 0.5 ppm is the same as 500 ppb.

The 2024 review summarises the levels recommended by the WHO and the US FDA for herbal products:

MetalLimit often cited for herbal productsWhat a good report shows
Lead (Pb)10 ppmA measured value well below the limit, or ND
Arsenic (As)10 ppmA measured value well below the limit, or ND
Mercury (Hg)1 ppmND or a very low value
Cadmium (Cd)0.2–0.3 ppmND or a very low value

These are ceilings, not targets. To see what a result means for you, multiply it by the amount you take. Example: shilajit with 1 ppm lead taken at 250 mg (0.25 g) a day gives 0.25 micrograms of lead a day. Smaller is always better, and no one should treat a "passing" result as permission to take more than the label says.

Red flags on a shilajit lab report

  • No numbers: "heavy metals: complies" or "passed" without a value for each metal.
  • Missing metals: only lead tested, with no arsenic, mercury or cadmium.
  • No batch match: the report is for a different batch, or has no batch number at all.
  • Old or undated: a report from years ago does not describe what is in today's jar.
  • No units, or the wrong ones: a result of "0.8" means nothing without ppm or ppb.
  • In-house only: the brand tested its own product, or the lab cannot be found.
  • Unverifiable accreditation: you can check a lab's status with NABL.

What else a good report covers

Heavy metals are the priority, but a complete certificate usually also shows microbial tests (total plate count, yeast and mould, E. coli, Salmonella) and, for many brands, fulvic acid content. WebMD notes that the FDA has not tested shilajit products to confirm that they contain what their labels state, and that shilajit's contents vary by mountain range, so batch-level testing matters.

Who should avoid shilajit

  • Anyone allergic to shilajit (mineral pitch, mumio). Operation Supplement Safety lists possible allergic reactions, raised blood pressure and dizziness.
  • Pregnant or breastfeeding women: it is not known whether shilajit is safe.
  • People with kidney, liver or prostate problems, or on testosterone therapy: speak to a doctor first.
  • Anyone on regular medicines: interactions between shilajit and medicines are not fully understood.

FAQ

What is a safe level of lead in shilajit?

Limits often cited for herbal products are 10 ppm for lead and arsenic, 1 ppm for mercury and about 0.2 to 0.3 ppm for cadmium. A good report shows values well below these, or not detected.

Is Indian shilajit safe?

It depends on the batch, not the country. Studies of Indian and Pakistani shilajit have found heavy metals above permissible limits in some samples, so only buy purified shilajit with a batch-matched lab report.

What does ND mean on a shilajit lab report?

ND means not detected: the metal was below the smallest amount the lab can measure. Check the report's limit of quantification to see how low that is.

How do I know if a shilajit lab report is real?

Check that it names an independent lab you can verify, matches your batch number, is recent, and gives a number with units for lead, arsenic, mercury and cadmium.
