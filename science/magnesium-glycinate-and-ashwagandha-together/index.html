Magnesium glycinate and ashwagandha together: is it safe?

Written and reviewed by Shubham Pasalkar, FounderUpdated 26 Oct 2026

Yes, for most healthy adults. NIH fact sheets list no interaction between magnesium and ashwagandha. The cautions come from each one separately: keep supplemental magnesium under 350 mg a day, and avoid ashwagandha if you are pregnant, have thyroid or liver problems, or take sedatives, thyroid, diabetes, blood pressure or immune-suppressing medicines.

Magnesium glycinate and ashwagandha are two of the most popular evening supplements in India, and they are often sold in the same tablet. This guide covers what each one does, whether taking them together is safe, what the only trial of a similar combination found, and how to time them. Every fact here comes from the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements (ODS), NCCIH or a peer-reviewed study, listed at the end.

Can you take magnesium glycinate and ashwagandha together?

Yes, for most healthy adults. The NIH ODS fact sheets for magnesium and for ashwagandha each list the medicines they interact with. Neither lists the other. Magnesium's interactions are with certain antibiotics, bisphosphonates, diuretics and acid-reducing medicines; ashwagandha's are with thyroid, diabetes, blood pressure, immunosuppressant and sedative medicines. So the pair has no known interaction of its own, and the safety rules are simply the rules for each ingredient.

Why people take them together

They do different jobs. Magnesium is an essential mineral; it contributes to normal muscle and nervous system function, and many diets fall short of the recommended amount. Ashwagandha is an Ayurvedic herb that has been studied for stress and sleep. The ODS summarises that several small, short trials found it reduced stress and anxiety compared with placebo, and a 2021 meta-analysis of five studies found a small but significant effect on sleep, larger in people with insomnia. Both are commonly taken in the evening as part of a wind-down routine.

What the research on the combination shows

There are no large trials of magnesium glycinate plus ashwagandha specifically. The closest is a 2026 randomised, double-blind trial in 103 healthy, physically active adults. One group took a morning supplement containing 600 mg ashwagandha with another adaptogen; another took an evening supplement with magnesium L-threonate (145 mg magnesium), L-theanine and apigenin; a third took both; a fourth took placebos, for two weeks.

The results were honest and modest. The morning ashwagandha group alone showed lower weekly perceived stress and better subjective sleep quality than placebo. The group taking both did not do better; on sleep quality, the morning-only group did better than the combination group. The authors note that healthy people over a short period may have little room to improve. The magnesium form was threonate, not glycinate, so the result does not transfer directly. The fair conclusion: the combination is not proven to work better than either alone.

Best time to take magnesium glycinate and ashwagandha

  • Magnesium glycinate: any time of day; most people take it with dinner or in the evening.
  • Ashwagandha: the NIH summary does not set a best time, and the 2026 trial above gave it in the morning. Choose one time and keep to it. Because drowsiness is a possible side effect, the evening suits many people.
  • Space magnesium from antibiotics: take tetracycline or quinolone antibiotics at least 2 hours before, or 4–6 hours after, magnesium. Keep oral bisphosphonates 2 hours apart.
  • Taking zinc as well? Very high zinc doses (around 142 mg a day) can interfere with magnesium absorption. Normal doses are fine; see zinc and magnesium together.

Adding melatonin? Read melatonin and magnesium together; it covers the extra drowsiness and driving cautions.

Who should not take ashwagandha with magnesium

  • Pregnant women: some experts advise against ashwagandha in pregnancy, and the French food safety agency ANSES advises against it during pregnancy and breastfeeding.
  • Thyroid conditions or thyroid medicine: ashwagandha might affect thyroid function; cases of thyrotoxicosis that resolved on stopping have been reported.
  • Liver problems: a few cases of liver injury have been reported with ashwagandha.
  • Men with hormone-sensitive prostate cancer: ashwagandha might not be safe, because it may raise testosterone.
  • Anyone on diabetes, blood pressure, immunosuppressant or sedative medicines: ask your doctor first.
  • Kidney disease: the risk of magnesium toxicity rises when kidney function is impaired.

How much is too much?

Add up magnesium from every product. The 350 mg limit counts magnesium from supplements and medicines (including antacids and laxatives), not from food. Loose stools are the usual first sign of too much. Labels often show the compound weight, not the magnesium itself; our guide to elemental magnesium shows how to work it out. For ashwagandha, the NIH notes that benefits in stress trials appeared greater at 500–600 mg a day, and that safety beyond about 3 months is unknown, so review long-term use with your doctor.

FAQ

Can I take magnesium glycinate and ashwagandha at night?

Yes. Many people take both in the evening. Ashwagandha can cause drowsiness, so the evening suits most people. Keep supplemental magnesium under 350 mg a day.

Is it safe to take ashwagandha and magnesium every day?

For most healthy adults, short-term daily use appears safe. Ashwagandha has been studied for up to about 3 months and its long-term safety is not known, so review longer use with your doctor.

Does magnesium glycinate interact with ashwagandha?

No interaction between them is listed in the NIH fact sheets. Each has its own medicine interactions, such as antibiotics for magnesium and thyroid or sedative medicines for ashwagandha.

Who should not take magnesium and ashwagandha together?

Pregnant or breastfeeding women, people with thyroid, liver or kidney conditions, men with hormone-sensitive prostate cancer, and anyone taking thyroid, diabetes, blood pressure, immunosuppressant or sedative medicines without medical advice.
